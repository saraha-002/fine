For men who work long hours in Nairobi, the idea of spending an hour crawling through traffic just to get to a club is a deal‑breaker. The Expressway changed that calculus. Suddenly, Kitengela felt accessible — not like a trip out of town, but like another neighbourhood. By 2025, daily traffic volume on the Expressway had surged from about 10,000 trips to roughly 65,000 trips on weekdays — a more than six‑fold increase.

2. The “Doughnut Effect”: Why Clubs Left the CBD

Kitengela‑based club proprietor Timothy Ole Timz Kasaine described what he calls the “doughnut effect” — patrons moving away from city centres to the suburbs and satellite towns[reference:0]. People stopped clubbing in the CBD and started partying closer to home or in accessible towns like Kitengela, where proprietors have bigger spaces to build entertainment venues[reference:1].

This isn’t just about distance. In Nairobi, high transport costs, heavy policing (especially alcoblow operations), and expensive parking fees have made city nightlife less appealing[reference:2]. Meanwhile, bar owners in satellite towns face fewer obstacles and can offer more affordable, relaxed experiences. The result? Men who used to fill clubs in Westlands and Kilimani are now heading south — to Namanga Road.
